# Rapid-acting insulin in English primary care, November 2020 to July 2026

Data pulled 30 September 2026. Every figure below is in `output/SUMMARY.md` or
`output/brand_concentration.csv` and is regenerated by

    python3 epd_source.py && python3 rapid_insulin_units.py && python3 compare_brands.py

## Question

The argument under test is that uptake of the ultra-rapid insulins (Lyumjev, Fiasp) has been low,
flat and uneven between areas, that this made withdrawing presentations commercially easier, and
that prescriber habit rather than clinical need therefore limits access. The data can speak to the
first half of that. It cannot speak to commercial motive.

## Data and method

The NHSBSA English Prescribing Dataset with SNOMED code, 69 monthly tables from November 2020 to
July 2026, pulled through the NHSBSA open data portal (`epd_source.py`). OpenPrescribing, which the
pipeline was first written against, serves the same dataset but refused scripted requests. The
dataset covers prescriptions dispensed in the community in England; hospital supply is absent.

Units are quantity multiplied by units per device. Quantity in this dataset counts devices (pens,
cartridges, vials), not millilitres. This was checked against the NHSBSA Secondary Care Medicines
Data for March 2026, which reports the same products in the dm+d unit of measure (ml): hospital
cost per ml multiplied by fill volume reproduces primary care cost per unit of quantity to within
2% for every device type, and reading quantity as ml would put costs 1.6 to 10 times too high.

The denominator is six branded rapid-acting analogues: Lyumjev, Humalog, NovoRapid, Fiasp, Trurapi
and Apidra. The original design used four. Trurapi was 7.5% of units in July 2026, nearly twice
Lyumjev, and leaving it out would have raised every share. Generic lispro, aspart and glulisine
and Admelog are reported for scale only; together they were 0.3% of items over the last 12 months.

These data are a census of community dispensing rather than a sample, so figures carry no
sampling interval. The uncertainty lies in what dispensing represents, set out under limitations.

## Results

Rapid-acting analogue dispensing rose from 6.39 billion units in the year to July 2022 to 8.07
billion in the year to July 2026.

| brand | share of units, July 2026 (%) |
|---|---|
| NovoRapid | 61.0 |
| Fiasp | 14.4 |
| Humalog | 11.4 |
| Trurapi | 7.5 |
| Lyumjev | 3.9 |
| Apidra | 1.9 |

The ultra-rapid share rose steadily across the window. Lyumjev and Fiasp together were 6.1% of
units in the first 12 months and 17.6% in the last 12, a fitted rise of 2.5 percentage points a
year over the whole window and 1.6 over the last 24 months. Fiasp accounts for most of it. Lyumjev
alone went from 0.1% to 3.7%, and its annual units rose tenfold, from 0.03 to 0.30 billion.

Variation between areas is wide. Across the 36 ICBs in place after the April 2026 mergers, the
ultra-rapid share over August 2025 to July 2026 ranged from 3.2% (Birmingham and Solihull) to 30.0%
(Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland), with a median of 17.2%. The 90th percentile ICB used 2.3
times the ultra-rapid share of the 10th percentile ICB (24.1% against 10.4%). Lyumjev alone ran
from 0.8% to 8.1% across the same percentiles. An ICB's Fiasp share and its Lyumjev share were only
weakly related (rank correlation 0.25), so areas tend to favour one of the two ultra-rapid products
more than they adopt the class.

Of 6,906 practices prescribing any of the six brands in the last 12 months, 3,960 (57.3%)
prescribed Lyumjev. The top tenth of those practices accounted for 42.6% of Lyumjev units, and the
Gini coefficient across them was 0.59. That concentration is typical of the smaller brands rather
than particular to Lyumjev:

| brand | practices prescribing (%) | top 10% of prescribers' share of units (%) | Gini |
|---|---|---|---|
| NovoRapid | 98.3 | 28.1 | 0.42 |
| Humalog | 90.5 | 32.3 | 0.48 |
| Fiasp | 88.3 | 35.9 | 0.52 |
| Trurapi | 71.4 | 45.8 | 0.64 |
| Lyumjev | 57.3 | 42.6 | 0.59 |
| Apidra | 53.4 | 44.2 | 0.58 |

Withdrawals are almost absent from these data. Every Lyumjev device except one was dispensed in
July 2026: vials, 3 ml cartridges, KwikPen U100 and U200, and Junior KwikPen. The exception, the
Lyumjev Tempo Pen, was last dispensed in October 2024 after 91 items in 22 months, never more than 8
in a month. Across all six brands the only other presentations to stop, or close to it, are the
Humalog Tempo Pen (289 items since October 2022, one in the last year) and the original Humalog Pen
(76 items across the window).

Vials and pump cartridges were 30.4% of Lyumjev units in the last 12 months, against 36.9% for
NovoRapid and 35.7% for Fiasp. The ultra-rapid share was 19.4% within vials and pump cartridges and
16.8% within pens and cartridges.

## Assessment

Low uptake of Lyumjev is supported: 3.7% of rapid-acting analogue units over the last year, behind
the biosimilar Trurapi. Uneven uptake is supported: a 2.3-fold spread between the 10th and 90th
percentile ICBs and a ninefold spread between the lowest and highest.

Flat uptake is not supported. The ultra-rapid share nearly tripled over the window and was still
rising at 1.6 percentage points a year in the last two years, and Lyumjev units grew every year.

The withdrawal claim finds no support in primary care dispensing. The only Lyumjev presentation
withdrawn is one that almost nobody was dispensed, and the devices most people use are all still
dispensed. A withdrawal driven by low uptake would be expected to show here as a device with
material volume falling to zero, and none does. This does not rule out supply problems, since
stock shortages would show as dips rather than stops and the monthly series above do not separate
a shortage from a change in prescribing.

Whether area variation reflects prescriber habit cannot be decided from these data. ICBs differ in
formulary, in how many people use pumps, and in how much insulin starts in specialist clinics,
which this dataset does not see.

## Limitations

Hospital initiations and hospital supply are absent. New starts on ultra-rapid insulin often begin
in specialist clinics, so the primary care share lags the true share by an unknown amount. The
Secondary Care Medicines Data would close part of that gap and has not yet been analysed.

Units dispensed are not units used. Stockpiling around shortages distorts the months near supply
problems.

ICB figures assign each practice to the ICB it belonged to in July 2026, because three of the
twelve ICBs merged in April 2026 were split between successors. Practices that stopped
prescribing before the merger (64 of 6,906, 0.03% of quantity) are left out of the ICB figures.

A brand with no rows in a month is counted as zero dispensed, which holds for this dataset
because every monthly table was pulled whole. It affects one month (Trurapi, August 2021, during
its launch at about one item a month).

The window starts in November 2020, when the SNOMED-coded dataset begins, so the first months of
Fiasp (2017) and Lyumjev (2020) are not covered. The older dataset without SNOMED codes runs from
2014 and would support the launch trajectories the original design listed as analysis 7.

The comparison of one month against the OpenPrescribing web interface, which the README asked for,
was not done because the site refused automated access. For the two presentations checked
(Lyumjev KwikPen U100 and NovoRapid FlexPen), the monthly sum of practice rows agreed with the
national total to within 0.04%.
