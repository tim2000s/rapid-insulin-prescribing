06.01.02.01    Biguanides
  
  • Metformin is the drug of first choice in individuals with type 2 diabetes with no osmotic symptoms
  • There is no evidence that metformin is ineffective in non-overweight individuals so it should be considered in this group
  • Metformin can be used alone or if necessary with any other diabetes therapy. It carries a low risk of hypoglycaemia.
  • GI side effects with metformin are experienced in up to 25% of patients, although only 5% cannot tolerate it. Modified release metformin can be trialled in these patients to see if tolerability improves.
  • Metformin should be started in low dose and built up gradually in 2 or 3 divided doses to a maximum dose of 1g twice daily with or after food
  • Metformin is renally excreted and accumulation may precipitate lactic acidosis. See Diabetes Handbook - Guidance on use of Metformin in Diabetic Patients with CKD for further information.
  • Metformin should be stopped in patients admitted to hospital with COVID-19 symptoms. This should be reviewed when blood lactate, renal function and hypoxic status are known. See ABCD guidance for further information.
  • Patients at home with COVID-19 symptoms should follow sick day rules and stop metformin accordingly. This should be re-started when well and eating and drinking normally.

06.01.02.02    Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2 inhibitors)
  
  • Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors act primarily by inhibiting glucose re-absorption in the kidney resulting in glycosuria
  • Due to their mechanism of action patients will test positive for glucose in their urine and there is an increased incidence of genital candidiasis which once treated, does not usually recur. Patients may also notice an increase in urinary frequency, equivalent to one extra void per day
  • The SGLT2 inhibitors cause modest reductions in HbA1c, blood pressure and weight
  • Of the two available strengths of empagliflozin, there are no significant additional benefits in using 25mg compared with 10mg, so the 10mg daily dose is recommended in NHS Tayside
  • See Diabetes Handbook for further advice on place in therapy of SGLT2 inhibitors and click here for advice on risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) with SGLT2 inhibitors
  • Hypovolaemia requires correction before initiation with an SGLT2 inhibitor and caution in patients for whom an SGLT2 inhibitor-induced drop in blood pressure could pose a risk
  • SGLT2 inhibitors should be withheld prior to surgery - please see local guidance for more information
  • Patients that are admitted to hospital with any acute illness should have their SGLT2 inhibitor withheld for the duration of the illness / admission
  • SGLT2 inhibitor therapy should be temporarily discontinued in conditions that may lead to fluid loss e.g. GI illness, in particular those on concomitant diuretic therapy
  • SGLT2 inhibitors should be withheld in all patients admitted with COVID-19 symptoms due to the increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) & hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS). See ABCD guidance for further information.
  • Patients at home with COVID-19 symptoms should also stop SGTL2 inhibitors. These should only be re-started after review by GP.
  • NICE Multiple Technology Appraisal 390 recommends that dapagliflozin and empagliflozin are suitable for monotherapy. Healthcare Improvement Scotland notes that NICE MTA 390 superseded previous SMC advice not recommending empagliflozin for monotherapy. SGLT-2 inhibitors can be used when metformin is contraindicated or not tolerated and a sulphonylurea is not appropriate
  • Empagliflozin and dapagliflozin are also approved by SMC as dual therapy in combination with metformin when a sulphonylurea is inappropriate, as triple therapy in combination with metformin plus standard of care, and as add-on to insulin therapy.
  • For information on the use of dapagliflozin in chronic heart failure, see formulary section 02.05.06.
  • Dapagliflozin has recently come off-patent making it the most cost effective SGLT2 inhibitor medication that we have on formulary. Patients requiring a supply of their empagliflozin whilst in hospital should have this changed to dapagliflozin 10mg daily. This applies across all indications and has been discussed and approved with the diabetes, heart failure and renal teams. Please ensure the patient does not have a history of intolerance to dapagliflozin prior to prescribing.

06.01.02.03    GLP-1 receptor agonists and GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist
  
  • The incretin hormones (GLP-1, GIP) are produced by the small intestine in response to a nutrient stimulus (meal). They act on the pancreas to promote insulin secretion in a glucose-regulated manner. The incretins also act centrally to decrease appetite, and they slow gastric emptying causing early satiety
  • GLP-1 RA therapy should be avoided in severe gastrointestinal disease such as gastroparesis or pancreatitis
  • For GLP-1 RA place in therapy, please see the Tayside Diabetes MCN Handbook
  • If GLP-1 RA therapy is being used solely for glycaemia, they should only be continued if there is a beneficial metabolic response (a reduction of HbA1c by at least 11mmol/mol and/or a weight loss of at least 3% of initial body weight in 6 months)
  • If there is underlying atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, GLP-1 RA therapy should be continued irrespective of the drop in HbA1c or weight
  • For GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist (tirzepatide), treatment should only be continued at 6 months if there has been a 5mmol/mol HbA1c reduction and/or 5% weight loss. Tirzepatide should only be continued at 1 year if HbA1c reduction > 22mmol/mol if not insulin treated OR HbA1c no worse AND total daily insulin dose reduced by > 50% if insulin treated OR Weight reduction > 8%
  • See Diabetes MCN Handbook - Diabetes and Driving for further information for drivers who are treated with non-insulin injections

06.01.02.04    Dipeptidylpeptidase-4 inhibitors
  
  • DPP-4 inhibitors enhance the effect of naturally produced glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)
  • Their advantage over GLP-1 receptor agonists is their oral bioavailability; however they are less potent at lowering glucose and do not have the beneficial weight effects or cardiovascular benefits seen with GLP-1 receptor agonists, although they are weight neutral and do not cause hypoglycaemia
  • DPP-4 inhibitors can be useful in frail older patients where a modest reduction in hyperglycaemia may improve osmotic symptoms
  • DPP-4 inhibitors should be avoided in acute pancreatitis and used with caution in patients with history of pancreatitis
  • There is limited experience with use of DPP-4 inhibitors in patients with moderate or severe heart failure therefore use with caution is recommended in these patients
  • Alogliptin has SMC approval for dual therapy with either metformin or sulphonylurea when either alternative is inappropriate. It is licensed for use in combination with other glucose lowering medicinal products including insulin, when these, together with diet and exercise, do not provide adequate glycaemic control
  • Sitagliptin has SMC approval for monotherapy (when both metformin or sulphonylureas are not tolerated or contraindicated), dual therapy (with insulin or metformin or sulphonylureas) or triple therapy (with insulin and metformin or metformin and a sulphonylurea)

06.01.02.05    Sulphonylureas
  
  • Sulphonylureas act by augmenting insulin release by the pancreas, so some capacity to produce insulin must be retained
  • Sulphonylureas are associated with a significant risk of hypoglycaemia and are associated with on average a 1-2kg weight gain
  • Sulphonylureas do not confer any cardiovascular protection
  • Sulphonylureas are generally no longer considered second line medications in most patients, but may be useful in certain situations such as:
    • BMI <25kg/m2 (providing type 1 diabetes has been excluded)
    • significant hyperglycaemia at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (providing type 1 diabetes has been excluded)
    • steroid-induced diabetes or where pre-existing diabetes has been worsened by steroids
    • certain rare genetic forms of diabetes
  • Sulphonylureas are principally metabolised in the liver and therefore must be avoided in hepatic impairment
  • Sulphonylureas may be used in mild to moderate renal impairment but must be avoided in severe renal impairment and used with caution in older people due to risk of hypoglycaemia
  • See Diabetes MCN Handbook - Diabetes and Driving for further information for drivers treated with a sulphonylurea

06.01.02.06    Thiazolidinediones
  
  • In light of age related risks with thiazolidinediones (fractures, heart failure, small increased risk of bladder cancer with pioglitazone), the balance of risks and benefits should be considered carefully both before and during treatment in those aged over 65
  • Pioglitazone should be avoided in hepatic impairment, heart failure (class I-IV), active or history of bladder cancer, uninvestigated macroscopic haematuria, older people, or high fracture risk
  • Prior to initiation with pioglitazone, urine should be checked for presence of haematuria (do not prescribe if present) and LFTs should be checked and monitored periodically based on clinical judgement
  • Pioglitazone may be prescribed as dual or triple therapy with metformin/sulphonylurea or monotherapy for patients in whom metformin and sulphonylureas are contraindicated or not tolerated
