# Why hasn't ultra-rapid insulin caught on? Nine years of prescribing data from the UK and beyond

*The usual preamble. This is analysis of published prescribing data and published trials, not advice
about which insulin anyone should use. The full method, every number and the code that produces them
are in the preprint and its repository, linked at the end.*

---

I have used the fastest mealtime insulin available since each one reached the UK, some of the time
on private prescription. I did that because I read the pharmacology and the trials and decided that
an insulin which starts working sooner was worth having. So I come to this question with a view, and
it is fair to say so at the start.

The question is a simple one. Fiasp, faster insulin aspart, arrived in England in February 2017.
Lyumjev, ultra rapid lispro, followed in October 2020. Nine years after the first of them, how much of
the rapid-acting insulin that people with diabetes are dispensed is one of these, and how much is
still NovoRapid, Humalog and their relatives? And what explains the answer?

## What a faster insulin is for

Every rapid-acting analogue has the same problem. Food starts raising glucose within a few minutes of
eating, and an injected analogue takes a good deal longer than that to get going. That is why we are
told to inject 15 to 20 minutes before a meal, and why so many post-meal spikes look the way they do.

The two newer insulins change the first half hour and not much else. Fiasp adds nicotinamide to
insulin aspart. In pooled clamp studies it appeared in the blood about five minutes earlier than
NovoRapid, with roughly twice the insulin in the first 30 minutes and about three quarters more
glucose-lowering effect over that time; total exposure was the same. Lyumjev adds citrate and
treprostinil, which open up the local blood vessels, and gets going about five minutes earlier than
Humalog with several times the exposure in the first quarter of an hour.

In the trials' standard meal tests, taking the insulin at the start of the meal gave a rise in
glucose one hour later that was about 0.9 to 1.6 mmol/L lower than with the older insulin in people
with type 1 diabetes. Lyumjev taken 20 minutes after starting the meal did about as well as Humalog
taken at the start, which is another way of describing the same thing.

That matters more than it sounds, because most of us do not pre-bolus. A Spanish study using
connected pen caps recorded 775 evenings from 49 people, and in 52.6% of them the dinner insulin went
in during the 45 minutes after the glucose rise from the meal had already started. The guidance says
inject first, and in real life the injection usually comes after.

People notice. Back in 2017 I ran an open collection of Fiasp experiences, and by 2024 it held 94
responses, nearly three quarters of them about how fast it acted. For this piece I went back and
collected 30 more public accounts from forums, Reddit, Bluesky and blogs, from 2017 to 2026. The
recurring theme on the good side is not having to wait. A Diabetes UK forum member wrote in 2021 that
Lyumjev "is faster for me, I don't pre-bolus now and I just feel much more confident with it." A
Bluesky user in 2026 described "no longer prebolus which has been a significant improvement in my
mental load."

The accounts were just as clear about what goes wrong. Nine of the 29 people described pain or site
reactions, seven of them with Lyumjev and most of them on pumps: "anything over a two unit bolus was
really too painful to use in the pump" (Reddit, 2022). Three described the insulin losing its effect
over the days a pump site was in, two had occlusions with Fiasp in a t:slim, and eight had gone back
to the insulin they used before. The trials agree. In the Lyumjev pump trial, infusion-site reactions
occurred in 19.1% of people against 6.9% on Humalog, and site pain in 15.8% against 2.8%.

So there is a real benefit, concentrated in the first hour and in the freedom to eat without waiting,
and a real cost that falls mostly on pump users.

## How much is actually prescribed

England publishes every prescription dispensed in the community, by practice and month, going back to
2014. Each line records the product and how many pens, cartridges or vials were supplied, which can be
turned into units of insulin. I counted every rapid-acting analogue: NovoRapid, Humalog, Apidra, the
biosimilars Trurapi and Admelog, insulin prescribed by its generic name, and the two ultra-rapid
products.

In the year to July 2026, Fiasp and Lyumjev made up 17.5% of rapid-acting analogue insulin dispensed
in English primary care. The other 82.5%, or 2.74 million prescription items, was one of the older,
slower products.

![Share of rapid-acting analogue units dispensed in English primary care by product, three-month rolling, January 2014 to July 2026. The black line is the combined ultra-rapid share.](fig1_share_by_product.png)

It has not been flat. The ultra-rapid share rose by 2.6 to 3.4 percentage points a year between 2021
and 2024. Since then it has risen by 1.5 to 1.7 points a year, and most of that slowdown is
Fiasp's. In April 2024 the Fiasp FlexTouch pen went out of stock, and it was later discontinued. It
had been carrying 36% of Fiasp's volume. People moved to cartridges, but Fiasp's growth fell from 2.3
points a year to under 1 point a year from that month. Australia lost its Fiasp pen in September 2023
and its Fiasp share fell from 9.5% to 5.3% the following year.

Meanwhile something else was growing faster. Trurapi, a biosimilar of ordinary NovoRapid, reached
7.5% of units within five years of launch. Lyumjev, after six years, is at 3.7%.

Hospital prescribers, who mostly means diabetes specialists, use the ultra-rapid products at about
twice the rate of GPs: 35.6% of what they prescribe against 17.6% in primary care. Hospital supply is
small, about 2% of the total, so it does not change the national figure, but it says something about
where these insulins get started.

## The spread between areas

The national figure hides a ninefold spread between English integrated care boards, from 3.2% in
Birmingham and Solihull to 30.0% in Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland.

![Ultra-rapid share of rapid-acting analogue units by integrated care board, August 2025 to July 2026.](fig2_icb.png)

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland publish the same kind of data, and the differences between them
are larger still. Over the same twelve months the ultra-rapid share was 14.7% in Scotland, 17.4% in
England, 26.4% in Northern Ireland and 32.8% in Wales. Within Scotland it ran from 0.3% in Shetland
and 3.0% in Tayside to 26.4% in Greater Glasgow and Clyde. Within Wales, every health board was above
the English average, from 18.8% in Aneurin Bevan to 56.3% in Cwm Taf Morgannwg, where more of the
rapid-acting insulin dispensed is ultra-rapid than not.

Outside the UK, five countries publish data detailed enough to separate Fiasp from NovoRapid. France
was at 14.3% in the year to June 2026 and Denmark at 11.0% in 2025. Both took up Fiasp faster than
England did and then stopped rising at around 11% to 15% several years ago, which is about where
England is now. Germany sits somewhere between 12% and 27%; its published tables lump Lyumjev in with
another lispro product, so a range is the best that can be done. Australia's subsidised prescriptions
were at 9.8%, without Lyumjev, which is not on its subsidy list. In the United States, in Medicare
and Medicaid, it is under 6%.

![Ultra-rapid share of rapid-acting analogue insulin by calendar year. Left: the four UK nations. Right: England against France, Denmark and Australia, with the German range shaded.](fig4_nations_countries.png)

So England is not unusual by international standards. The surprise is inside the UK.

## Why

### What the trials were designed to show

Every head-to-head trial of Fiasp or Lyumjev against its parent insulin used HbA1c as its main
outcome and tested whether the new insulin was no worse, within 0.4 percentage points. They all
passed. Pooled across nine trials, the difference in HbA1c was 0.02 percentage points. The one-hour
post-meal benefit was there, but as a secondary result.

That design is enough to get a new insulin licensed, and it gives prescribers little reason to use
it. HbA1c is what guidance, audits and incentive schemes look at, and an insulin whose headline
result is "no different on HbA1c" gives a busy clinician no prompt to switch someone whose HbA1c is
fine. NICE's guidance for adults with type 1 diabetes recommends rapid-acting analogues as a class,
names no product, and has not been reviewed on this point since 2015.

### The years when switching cost nothing

The trial design explains a lack of pressure to switch. In the first four years there was also nothing in the way of switching.

For the 52 months between Fiasp's arrival in February 2017 and the first biosimilar, Trurapi, in June
2021, there was no cheaper rapid-acting insulin to protect. The faster insulins were slightly cheaper. Across that period English primary care paid £1.68 per 100 units of Fiasp
against £1.76 for NovoRapid, and £1.69 per 100 units of Lyumjev against £1.76 for Humalog. Moving
someone from NovoRapid to Fiasp saved the NHS a little money.

In those 52 months, 10.1 million prescription items of standard-speed rapid-acting insulin were
dispensed in England, and the ultra-rapid share averaged 2.6%. By the time biosimilars arrived it had
reached 5.7%.

Some of that was formulary committees actively keeping the faster insulins out. North Central London
recorded Fiasp as "Not approved for: Adults with Type 1 diabetes" in November 2018. Birmingham and
Solihull restricted it to pregnancy, "after other insulins have been tried and failed", in June 2019.
South East London made it a second-line option in January 2020. None of those decisions could have
been about cost, because there was no cost difference to speak of. They were decisions to keep
people on the slower insulin by default.

Most of it was inaction. A prescriber renewing a NovoRapid prescription in 2019 had, at the same price, an insulin that let people eat without waiting 15 to 20 minutes, and in more than 97 prescriptions in every 100 the prescription stayed as it was. Hospital diabetes teams, which prescribe the faster insulins at twice the GP rate, still started most people on the older ones. The quality-of-life case was available from the
start, in the trials' own meal tests and in what people were saying, and the system did not act on
it while acting on it was free.

### Formularies

Each area has a formulary that sets which insulins are first choice. I read them for 17 English ICBs
and for every Scottish and Welsh board, and sorted them into three groups: open, needing a specialist
to start or recommend, and second line or excluded.

In England the pattern is strong. Where both products are open, the median ultra-rapid share was
26.8%. Where they are second line, restricted to pregnancy or not listed, it was 9.5%. Scotland
showed the same direction: the two boards using the Highland Formulary, which lists both products
without restriction, were at 26.2% and 23.5%, while the rest had a median of 5.2%.

Wales broke the pattern. Two Welsh boards, Cwm Taf Morgannwg and Hywel Dda, require a specialist to
start any mealtime insulin, NovoRapid and Trurapi included, and Cwm Taf Morgannwg lists Fiasp and
Lyumjev as first line alongside them; it has the highest share in the UK. Betsi Cadwaladr and Swansea
Bay restrict Fiasp to specialists while letting GPs start NovoRapid, and they still dispense 29.0%
and 45.0%. Whatever the Welsh formularies say, uptake is high. What no Welsh board does is require
the standard insulin to have failed first.

So formulary wording explains a good deal of the spread in England, some of it in Scotland and none
of it in Wales. Ayrshire and Arran and Grampian set no failure requirement and sit at 4.7% and 5.2%.
What is left over is something these data cannot see, and my best guess is the habits of the local
specialist service. A test is coming: from 30 July 2026 the new West of Scotland Formulary puts
Trurapi first and Fiasp and Lyumjev third across five boards, including Greater Glasgow and Clyde,
which currently has one of Scotland's highest shares.

### Cost, once biosimilars arrived

From 2021 the cost argument changed. Trurapi and the other biosimilars are about 30% cheaper than
NovoRapid and work at the same speed. Norfolk and Waveney estimated that moving 80% of people from
NovoRapid to Trurapi would save about £340,000 a year, and Cheshire and Merseyside about £1 million.
Dorset now pays primary care networks to get biosimilar aspart above half of all aspart prescribing.
Every one of those documents excludes Fiasp from the switch, but once Trurapi is first choice, an
ultra-rapid insulin becomes a second step, and the trial evidence gives that step very little to
stand on. Trurapi reached 7.5% of units in five years. Lyumjev, after six, is at 3.7%. By then the period in which the faster insulins could have become the default at no extra cost was over.

### Site reactions and supply

Site pain gives both the person and the clinician a reason to go back to what they used before, and
people do. In the Lyumjev pump trial, infusion-site reactions occurred in 19.1% of people against
6.9% on Humalog. On pumps, the faster insulins also come with shorter reservoir limits, six days for
Fiasp and nine for Lyumjev. The loss of the only Fiasp pen, in England and in Australia, made the
most widely used presentation unavailable at the point where uptake had been growing fastest.

## What Europe is losing

Neither product has lost its EU licence, but presentations are going. The Fiasp PumpCart, a prefilled
cartridge for the YpsoPump that we never had in the UK, is being withdrawn across the whole EU by the
end of 2026. The Lyumjev Tempo Pen was deleted from the EU licence in July 2026.

In March 2026 Lilly announced that it would stop selling selected presentations of several insulins
in selected European countries before 2027. Lyumjev is on the list, along with presentations of
Humalog, Liprolog, Humulin and Abasaglar. Since then Lyumjev cartridges have gone in France and
Belgium, its 200 unit and Junior KwikPens in Norway, and the vial, cartridges and standard KwikPen in
Switzerland. Germany has kept Lyumjev but dropped its five-pen packs.

### What Lilly and the regulators said

The formal statements are brief. The European Medicines Agency says Lilly "decided to stop marketing
some of its insulin medicines for commercial reasons", and that the decision "is not related to a
quality defect or safety issue". Lilly's template letter to European clinicians says the decision
came "after careful consideration and a thorough market assessment". In Belgium the wording is "after
a thorough review and a detailed market analysis". In France, Lilly said it was rationalising its
insulin portfolio worldwide to "maintain a continuous and reliable supply of the insulins most
commonly used by patients".

Closer to the ground, the reasons become more specific. Norway's diabetes association, reporting the
loss of the two Lyumjev pens there, wrote that Lilly "points out that these are variants that are
little used". In Switzerland, where the vial, cartridges and standard pen have gone, Lilly wrote to specialists in
August or September 2025 and did not inform the national patient organisation, diabetesschweiz, which
got hold of a copy unofficially. Replying to a patient's complaint in March 2026, diabetesschweiz
passed on what it had been told, and was careful to call it unofficial: that very few people in
Switzerland had stayed on Lyumjev long term, partly because of pain or burning at injection, and that
specialist prescribing had been very low.

That is the closest any source comes to saying it outright. Read together, these say that where prescribers did not use Lyumjev, Lilly found it easier to stop selling it.

It is not the whole picture. In the Czech Republic, the national regulator reported in July 2025 that
Lyumjev deliveries had risen by 20% in a year, driven by pump users including children, and Lilly said
supplies there were continuing as planned. And in the UK, nothing has been announced. The NHS
medicines dictionary marks only the Tempo Pen as discontinued, and Lilly's March notice covers the
EU and EEA.

### What "commercial reasons" means in money

It is worth putting numbers on "commercial". In England, primary care spent £5.6 million on Lyumjev
in the year to July 2026, against £18.3 million on Humalog. In France it was €6.4 million against
€49.3 million. In the US Medicare drug programme Lyumjev took $90 million in 2024, against $664
million for Humalog. These are what payers spent at list or reimbursement prices, which is not the
same as Lilly's income, but they show the scale: Lyumjev is between a tenth and a quarter of Lilly's
rapid-acting insulin business in each of these markets.

Lilly's total revenue in the first three months of 2026 was $19.8 billion, $12.8 billion of it from
Mounjaro and Zepbound. Its quarterly report does not list its insulins separately; they sit in a line marked "Other". A product worth a few million a year in each European country, with low use and
a reputation for stinging, is an easy line to cut for a company in that position, and the low use is in large part something the system created.

## What people are saying

The loss has not gone unnoticed, though it has been noticed unevenly. I collected 31 public posts from people with diabetes across Europe, and 11 statements from companies, regulators and patient groups, from 2025 and 2026. Every quote
below was checked word for word against a saved copy of the original. I have given platform, country and month, without names.

The sharpest reaction is from Switzerland, where Lyumjev disappeared first and with no public
announcement. One pump user wrote in March 2026: "Lyumjev worked extremely well with Control-IQ because
of the faster pharmacokinetics. It noticeably improved post-meal spikes compared with standard
lispro." The same person added, the next day: "There's no press release, pharmacy just says they
can't order it anymore due to being discontinued." Another Swiss post put it more simply: "Why was my
favorite insulin (Lyumjev) pulled from the Swiss market?"

A recurring suspicion is that insulin is being sidelined for weight-loss drugs. "Lyumjev is handsdown
the best insulin on the market at this time, its a shame Eli Lilly wants to take it away from many
people so they can focus on more profitable endeavours such as Mounjaro" (Reddit, May 2026). In the UK
the mood is uncertainty: "Doesn't look like they've said anything about withdrawing Lyumjev in the
UK, just EU/EEA, so hopefully they keep it here, but does seem worrying" (Reddit, UK, July 2026).

Not everyone is sorry. Site pain comes up again: "I was forced to use Lyumjev back when there was a
Humalog shortage and I hated it. Burning, painful infusion sites that left lumps several days after"
(Reddit, March 2026). Norway's diabetes association quoted a professor saying there are good
alternatives to every insulin being withdrawn there. Those reactions are real, and they are part of
why use stayed low. They are also the reason a choice of fast insulins matters: the people Lyumjev
suits and the people it hurts are different people.

## Why losing Lyumjev matters for fully closed loop

Fiasp and Lyumjev are not interchangeable. Lyumjev is widely regarded by people who have used both
as the faster of the two, and the evidence agrees. In a crossover study of all four insulins, Lyumjev reached half its early peak concentration six minutes before Fiasp. In the Cambridge closed-loop system, Fiasp added no
time in range over standard aspart, while Lyumjev added 2.5 percentage points over standard lispro, and
a pooled analysis of meals in that system found Lyumjev improved the four hours after breakfast where
Fiasp made no measurable difference.

That gap matters most where closed loop is heading. A fully closed loop, with no meal boluses at all,
has to catch a meal from the glucose rise alone, and the faster its insulin acts the less of the rise
it misses. Both fully closed-loop systems tested in UK trials were run on ultra-rapid insulin, Lyumjev
in adults and Fiasp in adolescents. The authors of a UK trial comparing Lyumjev with standard lispro in
fully closed loop concluded that "Further advancements in faster-acting insulins are needed to
alleviate the burden of pre-meal bolusing and enhance fully closed-loop performance in the future."
As one person put it on Reddit in September 2026: "every minute counts with a system working on a
time offset."

What is being withdrawn in Europe is the opposite of further advancement. Pump presentations are among the losses: the Fiasp PumpCart across the EU, Lyumjev vials and cartridges in some countries. Pump users
can still fill reservoirs from Fiasp vials, as the German Diabetes Society has pointed out, so the
faster insulins are not gone for pumps. But the fastest of them is going country by country, with low use given as the reason in Norway and Switzerland, at the moment fully closed loop is starting to need it.

None of this has reached the UK. But the UK uses Lyumjev at the same low rate as France, which has lost Lyumjev cartridges, and Lilly has not said what it plans for the UK.

## What I take from this

None of the reasons above rests on evidence that the older insulins are better. Each one is a default
that leaves them in place: trials built to show equivalence on the measure everyone watches,
formularies that put the faster products behind a hurdle, cost programmes that make a cheaper slow
insulin the first choice, and pump users who quite reasonably go back to the insulin that does not
hurt.

The part I find hardest to accept is the first four years. Between 2017 and 2021 the faster insulins
cost the NHS slightly less than the ones they would have replaced, and the system moved less than 3%
of rapid-acting insulin onto them. That was a quality-of-life gain available at no cost, and the people who missed it were prescribers and formulary committees. The people who would have benefited were mostly never offered it. The low use that followed is now part of the commercial case for withdrawing
Lyumjev in Europe.

The Welsh figures show it did not have to go this way. Across a whole nation, a third of rapid-acting
insulin is ultra-rapid, and in one health board more than half. I cannot tell from these data exactly
why Wales is different, and that is the question I would most like answered.

The evidence that would shift the picture is evidence on the outcomes these insulins are meant to
improve: how long people wait between injecting and eating, what their glucose does in the hour after
a meal, and which insulin they choose when they have tried both. The phase 3 trials that licensed
them used HbA1c as their main outcome and treated these as secondary or left them out.

## The limits

These are dispensing data. They show what was supplied, which includes stock held at home that has
not yet been injected.

The comparison between countries mixes different kinds of data. France and Denmark are national
dispensing in units; Australia counts subsidised prescriptions only and cannot include Lyumjev; the
US figures cover only two public programmes; Germany is a range.

The spending figures are what payers spent at list or reimbursement prices. They are not Lilly's revenue. The most specific statements about low use come second hand, through a patient organisation in each
case.

The formulary sorting was done by one person who had already seen the uptake figures, the English
pattern is confounded by geography (three of the five most restrictive ICBs are in London), and the
formulary wording does not explain the Welsh figures or the low Scottish boards, so whatever drives
most of the variation is not measured here.

The user accounts and community posts are self-selected and show the range of experience. They
cannot say how common any of it is.
