Why hasn't ultra-rapid insulin caught on? Nine years of prescribing data from the UK and beyond

Tim Street | Diabettech | October 2026

The usual preamble. This is analysis of published prescribing data and published trials, not advice about which insulin anyone should use. The full method, every number and the code that produces them are in the preprint and its repository, linked at the end.

I have used the fastest mealtime insulin available since each one reached the UK, some of the time on private prescription. I did that because I read the pharmacology and the trials and decided that an insulin which starts working sooner was worth having. So I come to this question with a view, and it is fair to say so at the start.

The question is a simple one. Fiasp, faster insulin aspart, arrived in England in February 2017. Lyumjev, ultra rapid lispro, followed in October 2020. Nine years after the first of them, how much of the rapid-acting insulin that people with diabetes are dispensed is one of these, and how much is still NovoRapid, Humalog and their relatives? And what explains the answer?

What a faster insulin is for

Every rapid-acting analogue has the same problem. Food starts raising glucose within a few minutes of eating, and an injected analogue takes a good deal longer than that to get going. That is why we are told to inject 15 to 20 minutes before a meal, and why so many post-meal spikes look the way they do.

The two newer insulins change the first half hour and not much else. Fiasp adds nicotinamide to insulin aspart. In pooled clamp studies it appeared in the blood about five minutes earlier than NovoRapid, with roughly twice the insulin in the first 30 minutes and about three quarters more glucose-lowering effect over that time; total exposure was the same. Lyumjev adds citrate and treprostinil, which open up the local blood vessels, and gets going about five minutes earlier than Humalog with several times the exposure in the first quarter of an hour.

In the trials' standard meal tests, taking the insulin at the start of the meal gave a rise in glucose one hour later that was about 0.9 to 1.6 mmol/L lower than with the older insulin in people with type 1 diabetes. Lyumjev taken 20 minutes after starting the meal did about as well as Humalog taken at the start, which is another way of describing the same thing.

That matters more than it sounds, because most of us do not pre-bolus. A Spanish study using connected pen caps recorded 775 evenings from 49 people, and in 52.6% of them the dinner insulin went in during the 45 minutes after the glucose rise from the meal had already started. The guidance says inject first, and in real life the injection usually comes after.

People notice. Back in 2017 I ran an open collection of Fiasp experiences, and by 2024 it held 94 responses, nearly three quarters of them about how fast it acted. For this piece I went back and collected 30 more public accounts from forums, Reddit, Bluesky and blogs, from 2017 to 2026. The recurring theme on the good side is not having to wait. A Diabetes UK forum member wrote in 2021 that Lyumjev "is faster for me, I don't pre-bolus now and I just feel much more confident with it." A Bluesky user in 2026 described "no longer prebolus which has been a significant improvement in my mental load."

The accounts were just as clear about what goes wrong. Nine of the 29 people described pain or site reactions, seven of them with Lyumjev and most of them on pumps: "anything over a two unit bolus was really too painful to use in the pump" (Reddit, 2022). Three described the insulin losing its effect over the days a pump site was in, two had occlusions with Fiasp in a t:slim, and eight had gone back to the insulin they used before. The trials agree. In the Lyumjev pump trial, infusion-site reactions occurred in 19.1% of people against 6.9% on Humalog, and site pain in 15.8% against 2.8%.

So there is a real benefit, concentrated in the first hour and in the freedom to eat without waiting, and a real cost that falls mostly on pump users.

How much is actually prescribed

England publishes every prescription dispensed in the community, by practice and month, going back to 2014. Each line records the product and how many pens, cartridges or vials were supplied, which can be turned into units of insulin. I counted every rapid-acting analogue: NovoRapid, Humalog, Apidra, the biosimilars Trurapi and Admelog, insulin prescribed by its generic name, and the two ultra-rapid products.

In the year to July 2026, Fiasp and Lyumjev made up 17.5% of rapid-acting analogue insulin dispensed in English primary care. The other 82.5%, or 2.74 million prescription items, was one of the older, slower products.

It has not been flat. The ultra-rapid share rose by 2.6 to 3.4 percentage points a year between 2021 and 2024. Since then it has risen by 1.5 to 1.7 points a year, and most of that slowdown is Fiasp's. In April 2024 the Fiasp FlexTouch pen went out of stock, and it was later discontinued. It had been carrying 36% of Fiasp's volume. People moved to cartridges, but Fiasp's growth fell from 2.3 points a year to under 1 point a year from that month. Australia lost its Fiasp pen in September 2023 and its Fiasp share fell from 9.5% to 5.3% the following year.

Meanwhile something else was growing faster. Trurapi, a biosimilar of ordinary NovoRapid, reached 7.5% of units within five years of launch. Lyumjev, after six years, is at 3.7%.

Hospital prescribers, who mostly means diabetes specialists, use the ultra-rapid products at about twice the rate of GPs: 35.6% of what they prescribe against 17.6% in primary care. Hospital supply is small, about 2% of the total, so it does not change the national figure, but it says something about where these insulins get started.

The spread between areas

The national figure hides a ninefold spread between English integrated care boards, from 3.2% in Birmingham and Solihull to 30.0% in Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland publish the same kind of data, and the differences between them are larger still. Over the same twelve months the ultra-rapid share was 14.7% in Scotland, 17.4% in England, 26.4% in Northern Ireland and 32.8% in Wales. Within Scotland it ran from 0.3% in Shetland and 3.0% in Tayside to 26.4% in Greater Glasgow and Clyde. Within Wales, every health board was above the English average, from 18.8% in Aneurin Bevan to 56.3% in Cwm Taf Morgannwg, where more of the rapid-acting insulin dispensed is ultra-rapid than not.

Outside the UK, five countries publish data detailed enough to separate Fiasp from NovoRapid. France was at 14.3% in the year to June 2026 and Denmark at 11.0% in 2025. Both took up Fiasp faster than England did and then stopped rising at around 11% to 15% several years ago, which is about where England is now. Germany sits somewhere between 12% and 27%; its published tables lump Lyumjev in with another lispro product, so a range is the best that can be done. Australia's subsidised prescriptions were at 9.8%, without Lyumjev, which is not on its subsidy list. In the United States, in Medicare and Medicaid, it is under 6%.

So England is not unusual by international standards. The surprise is inside the UK.

Why

What the trials were designed to show

Every head-to-head trial of Fiasp or Lyumjev against its parent insulin used HbA1c as its main outcome and tested whether the new insulin was no worse, within 0.4 percentage points. They all passed. Pooled across nine trials, the difference in HbA1c was 0.02 percentage points, which is to say nothing. The one-hour post-meal benefit was there, but as a secondary result.

That design is enough to get a new insulin licensed, and it gives prescribers little reason to use it. HbA1c is what guidance, audits and incentive schemes look at, and an insulin whose headline result is "no different on HbA1c" gives a busy clinician no reason to switch someone whose HbA1c is fine. NICE's guidance for adults with type 1 diabetes recommends rapid-acting analogues as a class, names no product, and has not looked at that recommendation since 2015.

In closed-loop systems the measured gain is smaller still. Pooled across 16 trials, ultra-rapid insulin added about one percentage point of time in range. It is worth noting, though, that both fully closed-loop systems tested in UK trials without meal boluses were run on an ultra-rapid insulin. The people building systems that do away with pre-bolusing chose the faster insulins.

Formularies

Each area has a formulary that sets which insulins are first choice. I read them for 17 English ICBs and for every Scottish and Welsh board, and sorted them into three groups: open, needing a specialist to start or recommend, and second line or excluded.

In England the pattern is strong. Where both products are open, the median ultra-rapid share was 26.8%. Where they are second line, restricted to pregnancy or not listed, it was 9.5%. Birmingham and Solihull, the lowest, allows Fiasp only in pregnancy "after other insulins have been tried and failed", and does not list Lyumjev. Scotland showed the same direction: the two boards using the Highland Formulary, which lists both products without restriction, were at 26.2% and 23.5%, while the rest had a median of 5.2%.

Wales broke the pattern. Two Welsh boards, Cwm Taf Morgannwg and Hywel Dda, require a specialist to start any mealtime insulin, NovoRapid and Trurapi included, and Cwm Taf Morgannwg lists Fiasp and Lyumjev as first line alongside them; it has the highest share in the UK. But Betsi Cadwaladr and Swansea Bay restrict Fiasp to specialists while letting GPs start NovoRapid, and they still dispense 29.0% and 45.0%. Whatever the Welsh formularies say, uptake is high. What no Welsh board does is require the standard insulin to have failed first.

So formulary wording explains a good deal of the spread in England, some of it in Scotland and none of it in Wales. It does not explain the low Scottish boards either: Ayrshire and Arran and Grampian set no failure requirement and sit at 4.7% and 5.2%. What is left over is something these data cannot see, and my best guess is the habits of the local specialist service, which is consistent with hospital prescribers using these insulins at twice the GP rate. A test is coming: from 30 July 2026 the new West of Scotland Formulary puts Trurapi first and Fiasp and Lyumjev third across five boards, including Greater Glasgow and Clyde, which currently has one of Scotland's highest shares. A fall in uptake there would show that formulary position matters even where the local service already uses the faster insulins.

Cost

It is tempting to assume the faster insulins are restricted because they cost more. At list price they do not. A box of five Fiasp cartridges costs £28.31, the same as five NovoRapid cartridges, and Lyumjev is priced like Humalog. I found no ICB document restricting either on cost.

The cost pressure is real, but it points at the biosimilars, which are about 30% cheaper and work at the same speed as NovoRapid. Norfolk and Waveney estimated that moving 80% of people from NovoRapid to Trurapi would save about £340,000 a year, and Cheshire and Merseyside about £1 million. Dorset now pays primary care networks to get biosimilar aspart above half of all aspart prescribing. Every one of those documents excludes Fiasp from the switch, but once Trurapi is first choice, an ultra-rapid insulin becomes a second step, and the trial evidence gives that step very little to stand on.

Site reactions and supply

Site pain gives both the person and the clinician a reason to go back to what they used before, and the accounts show people doing exactly that. On pumps, the faster insulins also come with shorter reservoir limits, six days for Fiasp and nine for Lyumjev, and a post hoc analysis of the Fiasp pump trial found glucose drifting upwards over each infusion set's life. The loss of the only Fiasp pen, in England and in Australia, made the most widely used presentation unavailable at the point where uptake had been growing fastest.

Withdrawals across Europe

The question I started with was whether low uptake has made these insulins easier to withdraw. In Europe, presentations are going. Neither product has lost its EU licence, but the Fiasp PumpCart, a pump cartridge we never had in the UK, is being withdrawn across the whole EU by the end of 2026. The Lyumjev Tempo Pen was deleted from the EU licence in July 2026. In March 2026 Lilly announced that it would stop selling selected presentations of several insulins in selected countries before 2027, Lyumjev among them. Since then Lyumjev cartridges have gone in France and Belgium, two of its pens in Norway, and the vial, cartridges and standard pen in Switzerland.

No manufacturer says this is about low uptake. The reasons given are "commercial reasons" and rationalising the range to protect supply of "the insulins most commonly used by patients". The German endocrine society called the PumpCart "very little used", and the Norwegian medicines agency said most of Novo Nordisk's discontinued products "have low sales and are not very profitable", although that list was mostly older insulins. Lilly is cutting Humalog, Humulin and Abasaglar presentations too, so this looks like both companies shrinking their whole insulin ranges, with the faster products cut alongside the rest. Within a shrinking range, though, the presentation fewer people use is the easier one to cut, so slow uptake can cost access without being given as the reason.

What I take from this

None of the reasons above rests on evidence that the older insulins are better. Each one is a default that leaves them in place: trials built to show equivalence on the measure everyone watches, formularies that put the faster products behind a hurdle, cost programmes that make a cheaper slow insulin the first choice, and pump users who quite reasonably go back to the insulin that does not hurt.

The Welsh figures are the most encouraging thing here. They show that the 11% to 15% level that France, Denmark and England have settled at can be exceeded: across a whole nation, a third of rapid-acting insulin is ultra-rapid, and in one health board more than half. I cannot tell from these data exactly why Wales is different, and that is the question I would most like answered.

The evidence that would shift the picture is evidence on the outcomes these insulins are meant to improve: how long people wait between injecting and eating, what their glucose does in the hour after a meal, and which insulin they choose when they have tried both. The phase 3 trials that licensed them used HbA1c as their main outcome and treated these as secondary or left them out.

The limits

These are dispensing data. They show what was supplied, which includes stock held at home that has not yet been injected.

The comparison between countries mixes different kinds of data. France and Denmark are national dispensing in units; Australia counts subsidised prescriptions only and cannot include Lyumjev; the US figures cover only two public programmes; Germany is a range.

The formulary sorting was done by one person who had already seen the uptake figures, the English pattern is confounded by geography (three of the five most restrictive ICBs are in London), and the formulary wording does not explain the Welsh figures or the low Scottish boards, so whatever drives most of the variation is not measured here.

The user accounts are self-selected and show the range of experience. They cannot say how common any of it is.
